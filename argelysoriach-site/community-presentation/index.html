The opportunity

Make the neighborhood the unit, not the subscriber

Current model

  • The unit is the subscriber.
  • The relationship is a weekly transaction.
  • Chefs earn a standard revenue share.

Commons model

  • The unit is the neighborhood: a node.
  • Each node is served by the kitchen hub nearest to it.
  • Chefs earn a higher share and a say.
  • A share of revenue stays in the node.

Hypothesis. Subscribers and chefs both stay longer when the meal belongs to a neighborhood.

Problem and solution

An opt-in tier. Not an overhaul.

Problem

Subscriber churn and chef turnover are highest in competitive urban markets, where margins are tightest.

To confirm with internal data.

Solution

CookUnity Commons: a subscription tier that customers choose. The core service does not change.

01 // Neighborhood by neighborhood

Each neighborhood has its own menu, delivery zone and chef roster.

02 // Two prices per meal

Standard is set to what the neighborhood can afford. Premium costs more and covers the difference. Ordering more meals each week lowers the price per meal in both tiers.

03 // Chefs and farms paid directly

Chefs earn a higher share. Every meal shows where its ingredients came from. Producers are paid directly.

Economics

The pilot must win back the margin it gives up

ChannelTraditional modelCommons pilot tierWhat the pilot must prove
Chef share Standard revenue share Higher share, plus governance Chef retention improves enough to pay for the higher share.
Mutual aid None Subscriber opt-in, with a 1:1 match option Grants, municipal partners or the tier price can fund the match. No source is secured.
Last mile Standard delivery Short routes from a nearby hub, by electric cargo trike Cost per delivery falls in dense zones.
Pricing structure Static platform pricing Dynamic node pricing (Premium & Standard rates) Premium tier adoption naturally offsets the margin loss of the localized Standard tier.

The break-even number per node is not known. The pilot exists to find it. Margin leaves in three places: the chef share, the mutual aid share, and the match. It must return in three: longer retention, lower acquisition cost, and lower delivery cost.

Pricing architecture

How the two prices work

Premium Rate

$18 / meal

(scales down with higher meal volume)

Costs more, pays independent chefs a higher share and covers the difference, so Standard stays within reach. It also supports the small businesses behind this neighborhood's kitchen hub.

Standard Rate

$12 / meal

(scales down with higher meal volume)

The access price, set from regional income so the neighborhood can afford it.

How the Standard price is set

Price(node) = Price(base) × (Income(node) / Income(metro))

Prices scale dynamically based on the area's median income compared to the broader city baseline. The Food API writes the same rule as standard_price_cap = ami_index × τ_threshold, where τ_threshold = Price(base) / Income(metro).

How the pilot finds break-even

(Price(premium) × Vol(premium)) + (Price(standard) × Vol(standard)) = Fixed Costs + Variable Costs

The pilot exists to solve for the volume required across both tiers to sustain the local kitchen hub.

Logistics

Each neighborhood is served by the kitchen nearest to it

Nodes

Five are modeled in COMMUNITY: Crown Heights, Bed-Stuy, Bushwick, Harlem, Lower East Side. Modeled, not built.

Kitchen hubs

Target: kitchens held in community land trusts, each cooking for the nodes in its reach. No kitchen is secured.

Cold chain

Electric cargo trikes for the last mile. No tailpipe emissions. Less exposure to street congestion.

Regional farms

Regional farm co-ops scheduled directly into the menu. No supplier agreement exists yet.

Pilot

One node. Five chefs. Measured against the baseline.

Phase 1 // Launch

A one-node pilot in New York: Crown Heights [01], five chefs, local farm partners.

Phase 2 // Measure

Against the standard platform baseline: subscriber lifetime value, acquisition cost, cost per delivery, chef retention, brand affinity.

Phase 3 // Expand

If the node pays for itself: a multi-node network, and applications for municipal food-security grants.
